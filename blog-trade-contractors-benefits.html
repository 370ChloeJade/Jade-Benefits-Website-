Health Insurance for HVAC, Electrical & Plumbing Contractors: Competing with Union Shops

By Jade Benefits | Fort Lauderdale, FL | Updated 2026

If you run a small HVAC, electrical, or plumbing company, you already know the hardest part of the business is not the work, it is keeping good technicians. Skilled trades workers have more options right now than they have had in years, and the union shops in your market know it. They lead every recruiting conversation with the same pitch: real benefits, real health insurance, guaranteed. Here is how a small, independent contractor can compete with that, without becoming a union shop.

Why skilled trades workers have the leverage

The skilled trades shortage is not a talking point, it shows up in the numbers every year. The industry needs an estimated 349,000 net new workers in 2026 alone, and 92 percent of construction and trade firms report real difficulty finding qualified people. Electricians see roughly 81,000 annual job openings with 9 percent projected growth, well above average, and plumbers and pipefitters see around 44,000 openings a year.

What that means in plain terms: a good technician today can afford to be picky. When two job offers land on the table and one comes with real health coverage, the decision is not close.

The union benefits gap, by the numbers

Union shops have built their entire recruiting pitch around one thing: reliable, standardized benefits. And the data backs it up. Only about 4 percent of union construction workers lack health insurance, compared to roughly 31 percent of non-union workers. Over 80 percent of union tradespeople have employer sponsored coverage with stronger benefits and lower deductibles. Measured in dollar terms, the average hourly value of a union worker's benefits package is 22.26 dollars, compared to 11.32 dollars for non-union workers, almost double.

Here is the opportunity most small shops miss: that 31 percent uninsured figure is an average across every non-union employer. If you are the shop that actually offers group coverage, you are not just competing with the union hall anymore, you are standing out from most of your non-union competitors too.

How a small shop competes without going union

You do not need union scale to offer union level benefits. Group health coverage can be built around businesses your size, even a crew of just a handful of technicians, and it gives you a kind of flexibility a union benefits fund simply does not offer:

  • You have real say in the plan design. The details, deductibles, networks, coverage tiers, vary by state and carrier, but you are the one shaping what fits your business, not a one size fits all union fund.
  • You keep your independence. No union dues, no collective bargaining agreement, no dispatch hall controlling who you hire.
  • You can lead with it in recruiting. "We offer health coverage" is a sentence that changes a hiring conversation instantly, especially against a competitor who cannot say it.

What group coverage actually costs a small trade business

This is usually the point where owners assume it is out of reach. It typically is not. Costs commonly run somewhere in the range of 300 to 600 dollars per employee per month, but the real number depends on a lot: your state, the carrier, your team's ages, and how the plan itself is structured. There is no single answer that applies to every shop, which is exactly why it is worth getting real numbers run for your specific crew instead of guessing based on what another contractor pays.

The real cost of losing a trained technician

Run the numbers on the other side of this decision. Recruiting, interviewing, and training a new HVAC tech, electrician, or plumber to the point they are fully productive on their own can take months, on top of the direct cost of job boards, background checks, and lost productivity while a truck sits empty or a less experienced tech works slower. A benefits package that keeps one experienced technician from walking to a union shop or a competitor almost always costs less than replacing them.

What this looks like in South Florida

Trade contractors in Broward, Miami-Dade, and Palm Beach counties are hiring into one of the tightest skilled labor markets in the state, with steady construction demand and no shortage of union and larger commercial shops competing for the same electricians, HVAC techs, and plumbers. Being based in Fort Lauderdale, we work with independent trade contractors across South Florida regularly, and we structure coverage around how your crew actually works, not a generic national template.

Trade contractor insurance FAQ

Why do union trade shops have an advantage in hiring?

Union construction and trade workers overwhelmingly receive employer sponsored health insurance and retirement benefits as a standard part of the job, while non-union benefits vary widely by employer. That consistency makes union shops an easy, low risk choice for skilled workers comparing offers.

Can a small HVAC, electrical, or plumbing company afford group health insurance?

Often, yes. Group coverage exists for businesses far smaller than a union hall, sometimes just a handful of technicians. Costs typically run 300 to 600 dollars per employee per month, though the real number depends on your state, the carrier, and how the plan is structured. A licensed broker can run exact numbers for your team.

Does offering benefits really help a small trade business compete with unions?

Yes. Since only around a third of non-union construction and trade workers currently lack coverage, offering a real group health plan immediately sets a small shop apart from most of its non-union competitors, not just union shops, and directly addresses the top reason skilled workers cite for choosing one employer over another.

How long does it take to set up a group plan for a trade business?

Most small group plans for HVAC, electrical, and plumbing businesses can be designed, quoted, and ready for enrollment within a few weeks. A broker handles the carrier comparison, paperwork, and enrollment meeting so it does not pull you off the job.
